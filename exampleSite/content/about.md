I grew up in the desert of Southern California, in a largely agricultural community, about as far from polar environments as you can get.

I completed my undergraduate degree in Earth and Space Sciences at the University of Washington in Seattle. It was there I took my first Earth science course and was captivated by the complexity of Earth systems. Polar environments became specifically exciting to me because of how fascinating glacier ice is. Ice is both a mineral with a crystalline structure and a rock that is sedimentary, formed by deposition of snow. Ice is a solid that flows, yet fractures, and that takes on different structures under different pressures. I had always been drawn to the logical problem-solving of math and coding, so modeling was a natural path.

During my Ph.D. at Georgia Tech, I modeled fluid dynamics at the glacier–ocean interface that are too small for climate models to capture. My dissertation explored how stratification, waves, and bubbles shape ocean flow near the ice and, in turn, how quickly glaciers lose mass. I'm drawn to questions about how ice shapes its ocean environment over short and long timescales, through mixing, water mass transformation, and geometric constraints. Looking ahead, I hope to extend my work into models of ice flow.

Some of the most rewarding experiences of my career have been mentoring undergraduate and graduate students, convening sessions at workshops, and hearing from community experts. Science is at its best when it's social, shaped by many perspectives and methods, and I love connecting with people over both its intricacies and its wonder.

That's also why I care about science communication, making work that is informative but accessible. Imagery is often the best way to do that, and during my Ph.D. I found a real passion for science illustration.
